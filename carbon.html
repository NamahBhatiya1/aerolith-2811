Carbon capture

The proposed pathway for locking CO₂ into stable carbonates.

How carbon capture works

This is the proposed pathway for the CO₂ side of Aerolith. It is a design to be tested, not a proven system.

Five steps

  1. Collect quarry dust. Acoustic agglomeration gathers fine airborne dust into aggregates that can be filtered or settled out.
  2. Mechanically activate suitable minerals. Milling cracks the crystals and exposes fresh surfaces, so magnesium-bearing minerals react faster.
  3. Expose reactive minerals to CO₂. The activated powder meets CO₂-containing gas. Moisture, temperature and contact time are conditions to test.
  4. Convert CO₂ into stable mineral carbonates. In the proposed magnesium oxide pathway, MgO takes up CO₂ and becomes solid magnesium carbonate.
  5. Separate and collect the resulting material. Filtration collects the solid, carbonate-bearing powder.

Why carbonates?

Carbonates are stable solids. Once CO₂ is locked into one, the carbon is held as mineral rather than as gas.

Where the magnesium comes from

Some quarry dust contains magnesium-bearing minerals. If ours does not have enough, magnesium oxide (MgO) would have to be added. Which case applies is something we still need to find out.

Where a MOF might fit Not validated

A metal-organic framework (MOF) is a porous crystalline material, and some are studied for taking up CO₂ selectively. In Aerolith a MOF could help separate CO₂ from other gases before or alongside carbonation. It is an idea to evaluate, not a component we know works here. The baseline is plain filtration.

MgO + CO₂ → MgCO₃

Magnesium oxide, where present in the dust or added as a reactive material, reacts with CO₂ to form magnesium carbonate.
