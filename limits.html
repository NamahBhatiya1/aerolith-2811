Limits and conclusion

What could go wrong, and why Aerolith still matters.

What could go wrong

These are the technical challenges and open questions we know about. Feasibility depends on how the tests turn out.

Dust varies

Mineral content differs between quarries. The dust may lack enough reactive silica, alumina or magnesium, so the binder and the carbonation may need extra materials.

MICP in alkaline bricks

Geopolymer is very alkaline, which can stress bacteria, and MICP also makes ammonium as a by-product. Both need checking.

Dilute CO₂

CO₂ in open air is thin, so how much can be captured is unknown. The reaction may work better in an enclosed or ducted stream.

Energy cost

Milling and ultrasound use energy. If it outweighs the CO₂ captured and dust collected, the net benefit is small or negative.

Acoustics in the open

Wind and open space spread sound and dust. Agglomeration may need a chamber or duct, which changes how it fits a real quarry.

MOFs, scale and standards

MOFs are unvalidated for this use, cost and scale-up are unknown, and bricks must meet building standards before anyone builds with them.

Research still required: characterise the dust, run the tests above, measure the energy balance, then judge whether it can work at quarry scale.

Why Aerolith matters

Quarries make dust and CO₂ together, and most fixes treat them separately. Aerolith asks whether one waste stream can be put to work twice.

Cleaner air

Acoustic agglomeration aims to collect the fine dust that normally escapes into the air.

Stored carbon

Activated minerals are proposed to lock CO₂ into stable carbonates such as MgCO₃.

Useful bricks

Collected dust becomes geopolymer bricks, strengthened by MICP, instead of waste.

An honest next step

Aerolith is a concept. The impact we are aiming for is only real once testing shows it.
